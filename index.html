PhD in Particle Physics

Universidad de Cantabria, Santander, Spain
(09.2019 – 03.2025)
Under the supervision of Luca Scodellaro, I successfully presented my thesis:
"Search For Supersymmetric Particle Pair Production In Final States With Two Oppositely Charged Leptons And Large Missing Transverse Momentum In Proton-proton Collisions At 13 TeV"

  • This thesis was focused on probing whether the data recorded at the CMS detector showed any evidence of new particles beyond the ones observed (known as the Standard Model).
    • In particular, the Supersymmetric model was tested, given its multiple advantages in answering the open questions in particle physics.
    • While no evidence of the existence of these particles was found, the stringest constraints to date were placed, thus helping reject many of the possible Supersymmetric models.
  • Whilst the goal of the PhD was scientifically motivated, its consecution involved applying and developing several data analysis techniques on very large datasets, using programming languages such as Python or C++, as well as Artificial inteligence (AI) or machine learning (ML) tools, Monte Carlo simulations and distributed computing with numerous parallel jobs on a large cluster.
  • The consecution of this thesis was also awarded the international mention thanks to my collaboration in the CMS experiment requiring me to 3-4 months each year physically at CERN in Geneva (CH). I also participated in several conferences across Europe and North America, and published multiple scientific articles.

    • Work Experience

    Instituto de Física de Cantabria (IFCA)

    Graduate Researcher (PhD Program) | Santander, Spain–Geneva Switzerland | Oct 2019 – Mar 2025

    While pursuing my PhD, I acquired the following skills:
  • Developed expertise in dealing with the very large datasets used in particle physics. This required acquiring skills in statistical modelling, data analysis, and distributed computing, and the understanding of the underlying physics, and the Monte Carlo simulations.
  • Presented scientific results to international research teams, honing communication and teamwork skills.
    • In addition to that, I was part of the CMS Collaboration, which spans over 3000+ scientists and engineers globally. Being part of such big collaboration required me to develop several teamwork and collaborative skills. These included:
  • Taking part in the testing of the next generation detectors (muon chambers) that will be included into the CMS detector, starting in 2026. This effort included:
    • Utilising the simulations of the full detector (calculated with the fluka software) to infer the expected flux of particles once the upgraded detector is in place.
    • Physically testing the behaviour of the detectors under close to real conditions (known as testbeams)
  • Validating that the Monte Carlo simulations used for the reconstructions of the collisions was accurate. This was achieved by studying the agreement between the simulations and data for:
    • Kinematic variables of the particles such as its momentum or angular distributions
    • Several machine learning particle identificators.
  • Participating in the internal CMS operations, including multiple internal meetings and presentations, resulting in an enhancement of my project coordination skills.

    • University of Zurich

    Research Assistant | Zurich, Switzerland | Jul 2018 – Jul 2019

    During this time, I developed my research skills by collaborating with an international research group before beginning my PhD. Tasks I did during this time include:
  • Assess how sensitive will the upgraded CMS detector be for some new physics (leptoquark) models. This required creating the simulations for the signal and backgrounds, and using tools such as Python and C++, and ROOT.
  • Studying the feasibility of using the CMS data for looking for less energetic signatures that could be masked by other less energetic events (known as underlying events, or pileup). This required creating simulations to understand the physical behaviour of the expected signal, as well as how could this be measured in the detector.
